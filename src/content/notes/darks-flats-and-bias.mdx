Anyone starting astrophotography runs into a strange instruction. After hours under the sky shooting a galaxy, you're told to shoot dozens more pictures of... nothing. A pitch-black frame with the lens cap on. A plain white frame. A frame as short as the camera allows.

These are **calibration frames**, and they are why a stacked deep-sky photo looks clean instead of blotchy. Each one measures a flaw of the camera or telescope, so it can be removed from the real photos (the *lights*).

## Why a camera needs correcting at all

A deep-sky target is extremely faint. To pull it out, you stretch the image's brightness enormously, and stretching shows *everything*, including flaws you'd never notice in a daytime photo:

- the sensor adds a little signal of its own, even in total darkness;
- some pixels are much hotter than others;
- the corners of the frame are darker than the middle;
- every speck of dust on the sensor leaves a soft shadow.

## Darks: the sensor's own noise

A **dark frame** is taken with the lens cap on, at the *same exposure time, ISO and temperature* as your lights. With no light coming in, all it records is what the sensor makes by itself: **thermal signal** (dark current), which grows with exposure time and with heat, and **hot pixels** that glow on every frame.

Subtract a stack of darks from each light, and those are gone.[^1]

With an ordinary camera like my R10, temperature is the tricky part. The sensor isn't cooled, so it warms up during a long session and cools as the night goes on. Darks are best shot on the same night, in the same conditions, right after the lights.

## Bias: the read-out pattern

A **bias frame** is the shortest exposure the camera can take, also with the cap on. In such a short time there's no thermal signal at all, only the fixed electronic offset and pattern the sensor adds every time it is read.

On their own, bias frames are mostly a helper: they are used to clean up the flats, and in some workflows the darks.[^2]

## Flats: the optics' fingerprint

A **flat frame** is a picture of an evenly lit, featureless surface: a light panel, a white T-shirt over the lens at dawn, or an evenly lit screen. It's taken *without changing anything* in the optical setup: same focus, same lens, same camera rotation.

Because the light is even, any unevenness in the flat comes from the optics. It records **vignetting** (darker corners) and **dust shadows**, little doughnuts from specks on the sensor. Dividing each light by the flat lifts the dark corners and erases the doughnuts.[^3]

If you refocus or rotate the camera, the dust shadows move, and old flats no longer match. That's why flats are taken every session.

## Putting it together

In simple form, each light is cleaned like this:[^4]

$$
\text{clean} = \frac{\text{light} - \text{dark}}{\text{flat} - \text{bias}}
$$

The flat is normalised first, so dividing by it only corrects the unevenness, without changing the overall brightness.

Each kind of calibration frame is itself a stack of many, often 20 or more darks, flats and bias frames are averaged into a *master* frame each. A single calibration frame is noisy, and subtracting noise adds noise. Averaging many keeps the correction clean.[^2]

Stacking software like **Siril** or **DeepSkyStacker** does all of this for you: give it the four folders, and it calibrates every light, aligns them, and stacks them into one.

## Is it worth the trouble?

Shooting calibration frames is the least exciting part of the hobby: no stars, no targets, just the cap on and the camera clicking. But it's the difference between a galaxy you can stretch and one that falls apart into gradients, dust rings and coloured speckles when you try. A few minutes of "nothing" at the end of the night can save hours of fighting in processing.
