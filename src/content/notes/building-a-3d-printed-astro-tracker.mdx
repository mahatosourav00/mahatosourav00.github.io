Earth spins at about 15 arcseconds per second. At 200 mm focal length, that smears a star across several pixels in just a couple of seconds. Long-exposure astrophotography is mostly a fight against that number.

> A good mount matters more than a good camera.

## The idea

An **equatorial mount** rotates around one axis parallel to Earth's axis. Once it is aligned with the celestial pole, tracking the sky needs only one motor turning at the *sidereal rate*:

$$
\omega_\star = \frac{2\pi}{86164.1\ \text{s}} \approx 7.29 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{rad/s}
$$

## Design notes

1. **Printed structure.** The gears and housings are printed in PETG for stiffness and heat tolerance.
2. **Stepper drive.** Microstepped steppers drive the RA and Dec axes through a reduction gearbox.
3. **Auto polar calibration.** The mount images the pole region, solves the field and tells me how far to nudge it.
4. **Guiding.** A small guide scope and camera watch a star. Any drift is turned into correction pulses.

## What I learned

- Backlash is the enemy. Preload everything.
- Periodic error from printed gears is real, but guiding hides most of it.
- Calibration frames (darks, flats, bias) matter as much as the mount.
